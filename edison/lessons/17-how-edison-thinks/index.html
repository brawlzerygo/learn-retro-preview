How Edison Thinks

Card advantage, tempo, which threat to answer, the order of your plays, and reading your opponent. 10 min · 10 flashcards

Cards are your resource

Count the cards you have: your hand plus your field. Do the same for your opponent. The player with more cards usually has more options.

Card advantage means having more of those cards than your opponent. Every card you play is a trade. You give up a card to take something away, or to gain something.

Think of it like this

Think of cards as coins. A good trade is when you pay one coin and your opponent loses two. A bad trade is when you pay two coins to take one of theirs.

One-for-one trades

Most removal is a fair trade. One of your cards for one of theirs.

Edison is full of fair trades. That is why the games feel balanced. One card that trades for two is a big deal.

Some cards replace themselves or give you extra cards. Players call these "plus" cards.

LP is a resource too

Many staples cost LP: Solemn Judgment costs half, Royal Oppression and Brain Control cost 800, Thought Ruler Archfiend costs 1000. LP you never use does not win games. Spend it when it buys you a card or a turn.

But watch how low you go. Gorz and Tragoedia punish an attack you cannot afford.

Your Normal Summon and your Graveyard

Tempo and card advantage

Card advantage is how many cards you have. Tempo is how much the board is doing for you right now: who is attacking, and who is answering. You can be ahead on cards and still be losing, if the cards you hold are stuck in your hand while your opponent attacks every turn.

Aggro decks like Blackwings trade card advantage for tempo. They put cards on the field early and attack before you can set up. Control decks do the opposite. They trade one for one and win once the opponent runs out. Know which one you are doing each turn. Ahead on tempo, keep attacking. Ahead on cards, don't rush, and make your opponent come to you.

Which threat to answer

You will rarely have an answer for everything. Before you spend removal, ask what the card will do to you if it stays.

  • Engines first. A card that makes more cards every turn, like or a in the Graveyard, does more over a game than one big monster.
  • Locks next. or can stop your whole deck. Your belongs on the lock, not on a random Set card.
  • Then the biggest attacker, unless you can survive it for a turn and answer it better later.

Sequencing: which card first

The same cards played in a different order can win or lose a turn.

  1. Removal before summons. Destroy a Set card with Mystical Space Typhoon before you Normal Summon, so a Trap can't meet your monster.
  2. Search before you decide. Use , or a Sangan search early. The card you find may change the best play.
  3. Bait with the smaller threat. Summon the monster you can afford to lose first, and keep your best one for after their Trap is gone.
  4. Attack with your least important monster first. Mirror Force and Dimensional Prison wait for an attack. Give them the worst target.
  5. Attack first, Synchro after. In the Blackwing turn in Lesson 19, the Synchro comes in Main Phase 2, after the materials have done their damage.

Don't overextend

Overextending means putting more on the field than you need. Edison punishes it. destroys every monster, every Spell and Trap, and and Judgment Dragon every card. Against a full field, one of them is one card for five of yours.

Put out enough to win, or enough to stay safe, and keep the rest in your hand. Two monsters that beat what your opponent has are usually better than four.

Read your opponent

  • Hand size. Many cards in hand means many options. One or two means they are close to out. Remember that , and work from the hand.
  • Set cards. Count them, and watch what they let through (Lesson 28).
  • The banlist. Mirror Force, Solemn Judgment, Torrential Tribute, Heavy Storm and Black Rose Dragon are Limited. Once one is in their Graveyard, there are no more.
  • Their outs. Ask which card would get them out of trouble, and play so that card does the least. If only Heavy Storm saves them, don't Set three Spells and Traps.
  • Their deck. Once you know what they play, you know what they need to happen. Know your opponent has every deck at a glance.

When to make a big play

A big play is a turn where you commit many cards at once, like a Synchro Summon followed by attacks. If it works, you get far ahead. If your opponent stops it, you lose several cards at once.

  1. Count their Set cards. Each one could be Bottomless Trap Hole, Solemn Judgment, Starlight Road or Mirror Force.
  2. Clear the way first. Use Mystical Space Typhoon, Heavy Storm or Brionac before you commit.
  3. Start small. Make your opponent use a Trap on a smaller threat before you summon your best one.
  4. Keep something back. A card in hand for next turn means one stopped play does not end the game.
  5. Go all in when you can finish. If the attack wins the game this turn, commit everything.
The big idea

Ask two questions before every play. What do I spend? What does my opponent lose? If you spend more than they lose, you need a good reason.

Common beginner mistakes
  • Making a Synchro every turn just because you can. Every Synchro costs materials.
  • Playing Black Rose Dragon or Heavy Storm when they hurt you more than your opponent.
  • Committing your whole hand into four Set cards.

Recap

  • Count hand plus field for both players.
  • Most removal is one card for one card. A card that takes two is a big deal.
  • Sangan, Card Trooper and Pot of Avarice give you more than they cost.
  • Synchros cost materials. Cheap Tuners make the trade good.
  • Before a big play, count Set cards and clear the way.
  • Tempo is what the board does for you now. Know whether you are racing or grinding.
  • Answer engines and locks before big monsters, and save hard answers for hard threats.
  • Removal before summons, small threats before big ones, and never more on the field than you need.
  • Count their hand, their Set cards and the Limited cards already used.
